Connect the pictures to the lecture
  1. Bounded response: x″ + 4x = cos t. The driving frequency 1 differs from the natural frequency 2. Both initial values are zero.
  2. Beats: x″ + 4x = cos(1.8t). Nearby frequencies produce fast oscillations with a slow envelope. For this fixed frequency, the solution is bounded.
  3. Resonance: x″ + 4x = cos(2t). The driving frequency matches the natural frequency. The undamped response has a linearly growing envelope.
  4. With damping: x″ + 2x′ + 4x = cos(2t). Show the two components: xc decays, while xp remains periodic.
  5. Final practice: compare x″ + 9x = 6cos(3t) and x″ + 2x′ + 9x = 6cos(3t), both with zero initial values. Predict which trial needs a factor of t before revealing the curves.
Why does the resonant trial need a factor of t?

With no damping, cos(ω₀t) and sin(ω₀t) already solve the homogeneous equation. Their linear combinations produce zero under the differential operator, so they cannot produce a nonzero driving force at the same frequency.

xp = t[A cos(ω₀t) + B sin(ω₀t)]

Substitution gives xp = [F₀/(2mω₀)] t sin(ω₀t). This is the growing envelope in the graph. The solution stays finite at every finite time; its amplitude grows without bound as time increases.

Revisit what multiplying a trial by x changes in Section 3.5.

What does the frequency–amplitude graph measure?

R(ω) = F₀ / √[(k − mω²)² + c²ω²]

This is the amplitude of xp = A cos(ωt) + B sin(ωt), so R = √(A² + B²). It is not generally the amplitude of the complete solution during the transient.

Positive damping keeps R finite at ω = ω₀. With weak damping, the response peak occurs slightly below ω₀; sufficiently strong damping removes the positive-frequency peak. In the ideal undamped model the formula has a singularity at ω₀, where a periodic particular solution no longer exists.

The displayed curves are evaluated analytically. Sampled paths are for drawing, rather than Euler approximations. The model assumes an ideal linear spring, viscous damping, and a sinusoidal external force.
